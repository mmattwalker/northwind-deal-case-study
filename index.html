Project Northwind

Should Hopper sign a white-label fintech agreement with a mid-size regional airline, and on what terms? A worked deal review.

Recommendation

Counter, and lead with the guarantee rather than the percentage.

As drafted the agreement is worth -$4.66M to Hopper and destroys $0.45M of joint value, so Northwind's $4.22M gain is funded out of Hopper's pocket and then some. Three changes, none of which touch the headline 40%, move it to $1.16M for Hopper and $0.86M for Northwind.

The deal on the table

Northwind Air carries roughly 2.4 million direct digital bookings a year and wants to white-label Cancel For Any Reason and Disruption Assistance into its own booking flow on a three-year term. It has asked for 40% of gross written premium plus a CA$4.0M annual minimum guarantee. Hopper funds a CA$3.2M integration and carries the underwriting risk, holding the claims exposure while paying a fixed share of the top line.

The question is sign, counter, or walk.

The revenue share rate is not the term that matters

The instinct is to negotiate the 40% down. That is wasted effort. Under the CA$4.0M guarantee, Hopper's NPV is negative at every revenue share rate, including zero, because the guarantee floor binds in every year up to a 30% rate.

What the percentage is a percentage of

Holding salvage at 55% and the guarantees at the counter levels, so that the only variable is the base the rate applies to:

31.9%
Highest rate Hopper can pay on net revenue after claims
50.1%
What Northwind asked for
40.0%

On Northwind's base Hopper cannot afford 40%. On the base Hopper should counter with, it can afford more than Northwind asked for. Same percentage, opposite answer.

The reason is risk transfer rather than arithmetic. A share of gross written premium is a fixed claim on the top line that does not move when claims deteriorate, so Northwind is paid the same whether the book runs at a 33% loss ratio or a 60% one. A share of net revenue after claims puts them alongside Hopper. There is a second, smaller effect in the same direction: premium is earned between booking and departure, so on a growing book written premium always runs ahead of earned premium, and a written-premium base pays Northwind on money not yet earned against claims not yet seen.

No single change rescues the deal, and the guarantee is why

Changing exactly one term from the agreement as drafted, leaving everything else as Northwind proposed it:

ChangeNPV to Hopper Improvement
Nothing (as drafted)-$4.66M
Revenue share base to net revenue after claims-$3.94M+$0.73M
Salvage recovery 35% to 55%-$2.04M+$2.62M
Guarantee cut to 1.5 / 1.75 / 2.0-$4.08M+$0.59M

Note the second row. Changing the revenue share base, the single most important term in the agreement, is worth only $0.73M on its own, the same as simply cutting the rate to 30%. While the CA$4.0M floor binds, the revenue share formula barely determines what gets paid, so improving the formula improves almost nothing.

Cut the guarantee first and the same change behaves completely differently:

The base change is worth $0.73M before the guarantee is cut and $3.59M after it. These terms are not separable and should not be traded one at a time. The guarantee is cheap for Northwind to concede and it is what stands between Hopper and the term that actually matters, so it goes first.

Terms I would push for

  1. Minimum guarantee cut to CA$1.5M, CA$1.75M and CA$2.0M. On base-case volumes it never binds, so it costs nothing in the expected case while still giving Northwind a floor to take to their own board. It bites only in the downside, which is the point of a guarantee.
  2. Revenue share base moves to net revenue after claims, defined as earned premium less incurred claims. Northwind keeps the 40% they anchored on, and conceding the headline number is what buys this.
  3. Salvage recovery set at a contractual minimum of 55%, with Northwind providing the residual value credit on refunded inventory. Severity is ticket x refund share x (1 - salvage), so this is their cheapest concession and Hopper's most valuable ask: it costs them a credit on seats they resell anyway.
  4. A loss-ratio corridor. Above a 45% earned loss ratio the revenue share rate steps to 30% and the minimum guarantee is suspended for that year. Both halves are required, for the reason below.

The corridor only works if the guarantee is suspended with it

This is the one drafting point I would not let through review. In the bear case the corridor on its own is worth exactly nothing:

Bear case protection NPV to Hopper
No protection-$2.32M
Corridor alone, guarantee stands-$2.32M
Corridor and guarantee suspension together-$1.10M

When the corridor trips, loss experience is bad, which means volumes and revenue share are low, which means the guarantee floor is already binding. Stepping the rate from 40% to 30% then reduces a number that is not being paid anyway, so the guarantee absorbs the entire protection. Drafted together the pair is worth $1.22M in the bear case. Drafted apart, the clause is decoration.

The exercise rate is the thinnest margin and the hardest to estimate from outside, so it is where I would want real data before signing.

What I would validate with real data

  1. Exercise rate by route and fare class. Two and a half points of headroom is not much, and this is the driver the model is least able to defend from public information.
  2. Realised salvage recovery on refunded inventory. The largest value lever, and its 55% assumption is illustrative. If Northwind's own resale data says 40%, the deal is close to breakeven and the other terms have to work harder.
  3. Attach rate on a white-label flow rather than an owned app. The Year 3 rate of 23% sits below the roughly 30% the consumer app achieves, on the reasoning that a partner-controlled flow converts worse. That gap is an assumption, not a measurement.
  4. Claim payment timing. Premium is collected at booking and claims pay out months later, so the real cash profile is better than this shows. That float is left out rather than claimed as a benefit it cannot yet size.

One number deliberately not counted

The 1.6x repeat-booking rate among customers who attach a fintech product is real and public, and counting it would add materially to the upside. It is excluded from the base case because it is a correlation, not a demonstrated causal effect: customers who buy cancellation cover may simply be the kind of customers who travel more. Treating it as a benefit of this deal would be assuming the answer. If a cohort analysis on real data separated the two, it belongs in the model, and it would be the first thing to add.

How this was built

The model is a Google Sheet, because that is where this work belongs and because it opens from a link with nothing to install. I built it with Claude Code: the Sheet's formulas were generated from a specification rather than typed cell by cell, and every figure was calibrated against an independent Python implementation of the same arithmetic until the two agreed to the dollar. The workbook carries that check in it, as a cell that must read zero.

That independent check earned its keep twice. It caught a breakeven rate I had first computed under two inconsistent salvage assumptions, and it caught a lever comparison whose rows each held different other terms constant, which made them look meaningful when they were not comparable.

This page is generated from the same calculation that reproduces the Sheet, so its tables and its calculator cannot drift from the model. The repository holds the memo, the assumptions, the generator and the rules the whole thing is built under.

Hopper facts used, and where they came from

All three are from the published job posting for this role. Hopper's actual margins, pricing and loss ratios are not public, are not used, and are not implied anywhere in this model.

  • Roughly 30% of consumer app customers attach a fintech product. Used only as the upper reference point for the white-label attach assumption.
  • Customers who attach are 1.6x more likely to book again. Computed as an upside bridge and excluded from the base case.
  • HTS is more than 90% of the business, with partners including Capital One, Air Canada, Uber, RBC and Nubank. Used as context for why a deal of this shape is plausible.
